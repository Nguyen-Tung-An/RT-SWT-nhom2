\subsection{Coverage is not test quality}
Our own data reproduce, on a controlled sample, a pattern that motivated this study's
focus on paired metrics: branch coverage and mutation score diverge. Baseline suites on
\texttt{JA-020} reach 60\% branch coverage while killing 0\% of mutants --- the suite visits
the target's branches without asserting anything that distinguishes correct from mutated
behaviour. At the aggregate level, the same divergence appears between RQ1 and RQ2: the
effective-subset coverage figures (75.0\% Python, 50.0\% Java) look far less alarming than
the corresponding mutation figures (36.84\% Python, 0\% Java). A reader who saw only branch
coverage would conclude gpt-4o-mini's tests are moderately competent; a reader who saw only
mutation score would conclude they are close to worthless. Reporting both, and reporting
both at the \emph{all} and \emph{effective} levels, is therefore not a methodological
nicety but a precondition for a non-misleading conclusion.

\subsection{Where does the ``breaking point'' actually sit?}
The original motivation for this study (GAP-T) was to locate the cyclomatic-complexity
threshold at which LLM test generation degrades. RQ3 finds no significant CC--quality
correlation within CC~5--10 in either language ($\rho \in [+0.13, +0.18]$, all $p>0.4$).
Read alongside RQ1/RQ2, this suggests the ``breaking point'' framing needs revision: quality
does not degrade \emph{gradually} across CC~5--10. Instead it is dominated by a
\emph{binary} outcome decided largely independently of CC within this band --- does the
generated suite compile, and does it happen to invoke the sampled method rather than a
same-named neighbour or an inherited overload. A function at CC~5 and a function at CC~10 in
our sample are roughly equally likely to fail this binary gate. This does not mean
complexity is irrelevant to LLM test generation in general; it means that within the
narrow, pre-selected medium-complexity band we sampled (chosen precisely to avoid the
extremes), the dominant source of variance is something else. Locating a true breaking
point would require sampling low-CC ($<5$) and high-CC ($>10$) functions for contrast, which
we identify as future work (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\subsection{Cost is not the bottleneck; correctness is}
Generating all 120 one-shot suites with \texttt{gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18} cost approximately
\$1 (amendment~v1.1, \S8.2) and completed in minutes; EvoSuite and Randoop, by contrast,
were run under a 60-second \emph{search} budget \emph{per function}, i.e.\ tens of minutes of
machine time for the same 60 Java functions, before measurement. On a pure
cost-and-latency basis, the LLM is two to three orders of magnitude cheaper. Yet on the
metric that matters --- does the resulting suite exercise and kill mutants on the intended
target --- gpt-4o-mini loses to both search-based baselines with the maximum possible
paired effect size ($r=-1.00$). This is the central practical tension our data expose: the
LLM's advantage is speed and cost, not reliability, and the two are not close enough to
trade off casually. A pipeline that spent the saved machine-minutes on a bounded number of
compile-and-retry iterations (a feedback loop, discussed next) would very plausibly close
much of this gap without approaching EvoSuite/Randoop's wall-clock cost.

\subsection{The INVALID rate as a finding, not a bug}
We deliberately did not filter the dataset to exclude functions that are structurally hard
for a one-shot external test to reach (e.g.\ \texttt{private} target methods): 17/60 Java
functions in our sample are \texttt{private}, and removing them post hoc --- after already
observing that Java's effective rate is low --- would have amounted to selecting the sample
based on the outcome we were trying to measure, undermining the pre-registered protocol
(amendments~v1.1/v1.2, \S\ref{sec:threats}). The decision paid off empirically in an
unexpected way: visibility turns out \emph{not} to be the dominant cause. Only 1 of the 9
truly non-compiling Java functions, and only 14 of the 46 Java functions that compiled but
touched nothing, involve a \texttt{private} target (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Had we
filtered on visibility we would have removed the wrong variable and left the dominant
failure mode --- wrong-target invocation via overload/name resolution, clustering around
API-heavy classes such as \texttt{AccurateMath} and \texttt{Gson} --- completely
unexplained.

This pattern is not unique to our sample. Haratian et al.\ (2026) report a 63\%
compilation-error rate for GPT-4o on 353 real Java pull requests, roughly half our Java
\textsc{invalid}-or-no-touch rate (91.7\%) but the same qualitative story: one-shot,
zero-context generation on real (not curated) code compiles far less reliably than
benchmark-style evaluations suggest. Sapozhnikov et al.\ (2024) similarly report over 50\%
malformed/non-compiling ChatGPT-generated Java tests, and Kumar et al.\ (2025) attribute
weak single-pass results directly to the \emph{absence of an iterative-repair loop}. The
techniques the wider literature uses to close this gap --- feeding compiler/test-failure
output back to the model (Zeng et al., 2026; Xue et al., 2025), retrieving real API
signatures instead of raw source text (addressing exactly the wrong-target-invocation
pattern we observe), or sampling multiple candidates and keeping the one that compiles ---
all require leaving the strict one-shot setting we evaluate here. Our results should
therefore be read as a lower bound on what GPT-4o-mini can do with this codebase, and as
direct motivation for why the literature has moved toward feedback-augmented and
retrieval-augmented pipelines rather than static one-shot prompting.

\subsection{Why gpt-4o-mini ``beats'' Pynguin}
The RQ2-B result against Pynguin ($r=+0.85$, gpt-4o-mini favoured) illustrates a general
risk in baseline comparisons: a win can reflect the baseline's tooling difficulty rather
than the treatment's strength. Pynguin's own median branch coverage on this dataset is
0.0\% (Section~\ref{sec:threats}), driven by search timeouts and a \texttt{dill}
serialization failure on modules importing \texttt{\_json} --- issues orthogonal to
gpt-4o-mini's test-writing ability. We report the comparison because it is the
pre-registered baseline for Python (amendment~v1.2) and because suppressing an inconvenient
result would itself be a validity threat, but we caution against reading it as evidence that
gpt-4o-mini produces good Python tests in absolute terms; RQ1/RQ2-A already show it does
not.

\subsection{RQ4 (exploratory, post-hoc): does real API context help?}
\label{sec:rq4}
Motivated by the wrong-target-invocation pattern above, we ran a small \emph{exploratory,
post-hoc} follow-up: the same 120 functions, same model/temperature/exemplar, but with the
target class's (Java) or module's (Python) real public-API skeleton --- method signatures,
nested-class structure, module-level-function-vs-class-method distinction, extracted
directly from the pinned source with \texttt{javalang}/\texttt{ast} --- inserted into the
one-shot prompt. This is explicitly \emph{not} part of the pre-registered RQ1--RQ3: it is
reported here, separately, because the idea arose only after seeing the N=120 results, and
folding it into the confirmatory analysis would be HARKing. The new tests were generated
into a separate directory and measured into a separate results file, never overwriting the
registered N=120 data.

\textbf{Java.} The compile rate is unchanged (51/60 both arms), but the \emph{effective}
rate --- suites that actually exercise the target --- more than doubles: 11/60 (18.3\%)
with context vs.\ 5/60 (8.3\%) without. Paired by function, 7/60 improve, 1/60 regresses,
52/60 are unchanged; a one-sided paired Wilcoxon test is not significant at $\alpha=0.05$ but
close (7 vs.\ 1 favourable pairs is nonetheless suggestive) and the effect size among the
non-tied pairs is moderate-to-large. Mutation score shows no improvement ($n=48$ paired,
both medians 0\%). We read this as a promising but inconclusive signal that context reduces
wrong-target invocation (its intended target) without yet translating into stronger
fault-detection --- consistent with context fixing a \emph{structural knowledge} gap
(which method to call) rather than a \emph{behavioural reasoning} gap (what the method
actually does), the latter being what mutation score measures.

\textbf{Python.} On the pre-registered whole-suite-must-pass criterion, context looks
\emph{worse} (7/60 vs.\ 32/60 compiled) --- but this is a measurement artefact, not evidence
that context hurts. Counting individual test functions rather than whole files, the two
arms are statistically indistinguishable (267 test cases, 33.3\% passing, with context vs.\
233 test cases, 32.2\% passing, without). Context leads gpt-4o-mini to write \emph{more}
test cases per file (a plausible effect of seeing more of the API surface), and because our
Python \textsc{invalid} criterion requires every test in a file to pass, more tests per file
mechanically increases the chance that at least one fails on an unrelated behavioural bug ---
we traced one such case (\texttt{flask.logging.has\_level\_handler}) to an identical
misunderstanding of logger-hierarchy propagation present in \emph{both} arms, unrelated to
context. This illustrates a second-order threat to validity worth flagging for future work:
an all-or-nothing pass criterion penalises test-suite thoroughness whenever any one
assertion is wrong, and can make two arms with near-identical per-test correctness look
dramatically different.

\textbf{Implication.} Static API context is a low-cost intervention (no additional model
calls beyond the original one-shot budget) that shows a real, if not yet significant, effect
on Java's wrong-target problem, and a neutral effect on Python once measured fairly. Neither
result changes the RQ1--RQ3 conclusions (Section~\ref{sec:results}): gpt-4o-mini one-shot
generation, with or without context, remains well short of the 80\%/60\% thresholds.

\subsection{RQ5 (exploratory, post-hoc): one feedback/repair iteration on top of RQ4}
\label{sec:rq5}
As a single, pre-committed follow-up to RQ4 --- decided \emph{before} running it, and run
\emph{once}, per our own anti-HARKing discipline (we do not keep iterating techniques until
a threshold is crossed) --- we fed each RQ4 suite that was not yet effective back to
gpt-4o-mini together with a real failure signal (Java: the 0\%-coverage outcome and the
target's API skeleton, since Maven/JVM error logs proved too noisy to use as a clean
compiler-diagnostic signal, Section~\ref{sec:threats}; Python: the actual fresh
\texttt{pytest} failure output) and asked for one corrective rewrite. Suites already
effective at RQ4 (11/60 Java, matching Python's per-test baseline) were carried forward
unchanged rather than re-generated.

\textbf{Java: further, still-inconclusive improvement.} The effective rate rises again, from
11/60 (18.3\%, RQ4) to \textbf{15/60 (25.0\%)}. Critically, of the functions that changed, 4
improved and \emph{none regressed} (repair $\geq$ context on every paired function); the
one-sided paired Wilcoxon test gives $p=0.063$ --- still short of $\alpha=0.05$ at this
sample size, but with the maximum possible rank-biserial effect size ($r=1.00$, since every
non-tied pair favours repair) and a monotonic improvement across three stages (8.3\% $\to$
18.3\% $\to$ 25.0\%). Mutation score remains flat at a median of 0\% ($n=45$ paired
functions), unchanged from RQ4: repair helps gpt-4o-mini hit the right lines more often, but,
as with context alone, does not yet translate into assertions strong enough to kill mutants.

\textbf{Python: a genuine regression, not a measurement artefact this time.} Per-test-case
pass rate \emph{drops} to 15.5\% (106/682 tests), well below both RQ4 (33.3\%) and the
original no-context arm (32.2\%). Unlike the RQ4-vs-original comparison, this is not an
artefact of file count or suite length alone: test volume grew again (682 tests across 60
files, vs.\ 267 at RQ4), consistent with gpt-4o-mini responding to a visible failure by
\emph{expanding} the suite (adding more cases) rather than narrowly fixing the specific
assertion that failed, and the additional cases fail at a higher rate than the original ones
survived. We do not have a definitive mechanism for this and did not run further diagnostics
to find one, consistent with our commitment to run this technique once and report the
outcome rather than keep adjusting until it looks better.

\textbf{Overall verdict on RQ4+RQ5.} Across two rounds of low-cost, non-fine-tuning
intervention, Java shows a real (if not significance-clearing) upward trend on coverage
effectiveness and no downside; Python shows no benefit from context and a real regression
from single-shot repair. Neither technique, alone or combined, closes the gap to the
pre-registered 80\%/60\% thresholds (Section~\ref{sec:results}): the median across the full
$N=120$ sample remains 0\% for both metrics at every stage we tried. This is itself a
finding: the specific interventions the literature suggests as next steps after one-shot
prompting (Section~\ref{sec:related}) are not a guaranteed fix, help unevenly across
languages, and in at least one case (Python repair) can make matters worse --- a caution
against assuming that ``add more scaffolding'' straightforwardly improves LLM test
generation. Per our agreement not to keep searching for a technique that crosses the
threshold, we stop here rather than attempt a third intervention.
