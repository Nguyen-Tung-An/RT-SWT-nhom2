Unit testing remains one of the most expensive activities in software
development, frequently cited as consuming 30--50\% of total project
effort. Large Language Models (LLMs) promise to reduce this cost by
generating unit tests directly from source code, and recent studies
report encouraging results: GPT-4-class models achieve high line
coverage on curated benchmarks such as TestEval and HumanEval.
However, most of these evaluations concentrate on relatively simple,
self-contained functions, and they rarely control for the structural
complexity of the code under test.

This leaves two gaps that our study addresses. First, existing work
does not systematically examine \emph{where LLM-generated tests break
down} as cyclomatic complexity (CC) increases; results are typically
aggregated over functions of mixed, unreported complexity
(GAP-T). Second, few studies combine \emph{branch coverage} and
\emph{mutation score} under statistical testing on the same controlled
sample, even though coverage alone is known to overestimate test
quality (GAP-M) --- an effect we also observe directly in our own
baseline data, where a function can reach 60\% branch coverage while
killing 0\% of mutants (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

We therefore evaluate \textbf{GPT-4o-mini} (one-shot prompting,
temperature $0$) on \textbf{120 real-world functions} (60 Java, 60
Python) mined from ten pinned open-source repositories, all within the
medium-complexity band $5 \le \mathrm{CC} \le 10$. Generated tests are
executed \emph{in the context of the original projects}, and compared
against two automated baselines --- EvoSuite and Randoop --- under an
equal 60-second generation budget per class. Quality is measured by
branch coverage (JaCoCo, coverage.py) and mutation score (PIT, an
AST-based mutation engine for Python), with non-parametric statistical
tests at $\alpha = 0.05$.

\textbf{RQ1}: Does GPT-4o-mini achieve $\geq 80\%$ branch coverage on functions
of medium cyclomatic complexity? \\
\textbf{RQ2}: Does GPT-4o-mini achieve $\geq 60\%$ mutation score, and does it
outperform automated baselines? \\
\textbf{RQ3}: Is there a negative correlation between cyclomatic complexity and
test quality?

The contributions of this paper are: (i) a reproducible benchmark of
120 medium-complexity functions with pinned provenance; (ii) a
measurement pipeline that scores tests \emph{per function} inside the
original projects, with a mandatory green-on-original check before
mutation analysis; (iii) an empirical comparison of GPT-4o-mini
against search-based and random baselines under equal budgets, showing
it loses decisively to search-based Java tools (EvoSuite, Randoop;
paired rank-biserial $r=-1.00$) while both GPT-4o-mini and every
baseline fall well short of the 80\%/60\% thresholds on this
medium-complexity sample; and (iv) a catalogue of measurement pitfalls
(silent tool failures, coverage misattribution) that materially affect
reported numbers if left undetected.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work;
Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the dataset, generation, and
measurement methodology; Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results per
research question; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses
implications; Section~\ref{sec:threats} examines threats to validity;
and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
