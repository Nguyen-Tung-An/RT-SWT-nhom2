\subsection{LLM-based test generation on curated benchmarks}
A large share of recent work reports strong results for GPT-4-class models on curated,
self-contained benchmarks. Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2025testeval} report GPT-4o reaching 98.65\% line and
97.16\% branch coverage on TestEval (Python, LeetCode-style problems); Kumar et al.\ \cite{kumar2025empirical}
report 99.05\% branch coverage for a mix of Gemini/GPT-4o/Claude-3.5 on HumanEval-Java; Lira
et al.\ \cite{lira2025evaluating} report a 90.69\% hit rate for Gemini~1.5-Pro with source and docstring
context on HumanEval. These benchmarks share two properties directly relevant to our
design: functions are short, single-file, and largely self-contained (no real project
context to resolve), and complexity is not controlled or reported. Chudic and \c{C}al{\i}kl{\i}
\cite{chudic2026enhancement} and Jasti et al.\ \cite{jasti2026feedback} similarly report high coverage on HumanEval/ClassEval-style
problems. This class of result motivates the first half of our GAP statement (GAP-T): high
performance on curated, low-complexity problems does not establish how the same models
behave on medium-complexity, real-project code.

\subsection{Degradation on real-world code}
A second, smaller cluster measures LLM test generation directly on real projects and
reports substantially weaker results. Haratian et al.\ \cite{haratian2026praware} evaluate GPT-4o on 353 real
Java pull requests and report a 63\% compilation-error rate and only 13\% fail-to-pass
effectiveness (vs.\ 36\% for EvoSuite on the same PRs). Sapozhnikov et al.\ \cite{sapozhnikov2024empirical} report
that over 50\% of ChatGPT-generated Java tests are malformed or non-compiling, attributing
this partly to prompt-size limits forcing incomplete context. Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2024testbench}
(TestBench) report Java compile-error rates of 47.9--55.9\% across CodeLlama,
GPT-3.5, and GPT-4 on nine open-source projects. Konstantinou et al.\ \cite{konstantinou2026evaluating} find that a
plain GPT-4o-mini pipeline produces many non-compiling/non-passing tests across six Java
projects, with mutation score falling to 33.82\% --- notably the same model we evaluate.
Jain and Le Goues \cite{jain2025testgeneval} report a mutation score of only 33.8\% for GPT-4o on TestGenEval
(Python), despite an 84.3\% pass@1. Our Java \textsc{invalid}-or-no-touch rate (91.7\%) and
Python \textsc{invalid} rate (46.7\%) are more severe than most of these figures, which we
attribute to two compounding factors largely absent from the cited studies: (i) we measure
tests \emph{inside the real installed project} rather than in isolation, so a test must
resolve real imports, real class hierarchies, and real method signatures to count as
effective, and (ii) we use a lighter model (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}) than the \texttt{gpt-4o}/
\texttt{GPT-4-Turbo} evaluated in most of the cited work. Both choices make our setting
harder by design, consistent with our goal of measuring a lower bound rather than a
best-case capability.

\subsection{Automated (non-LLM) baselines}
Search-based and random test generation give us a non-LLM point of comparison. EvoSuite
\cite{fraser2011evosuite} uses a genetic-algorithm search over test suites and is widely used as an SBST baseline in
this literature (e.g.\ Ouédraogo et al.\ \cite{ouedraogo2025prompt}; Gu et al.\ \cite{gu2025template}; Broide et al.\ \cite{broide2026evogpt}, all on
Defects4J); Randoop \cite{pacheco2007randoop} instead performs feedback-directed random testing. Both are deterministic
in their \emph{approach} (not in the tests they emit) and do not depend on natural-language
prompting, so their coverage/mutation numbers are not subject to the compile-time failure
modes we observe for the LLM. On the Python side, Pynguin \cite{lukasczyk2022pynguin} is the closest analogue to
EvoSuite, using the DYNAMOSA many-objective search algorithm; it is used as a baseline or
point of comparison in, e.g., Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2026testdecision} and Konstantinou et al.\ \cite{konstantinou2025yate}, typically
alongside or against LLM-based approaches. We use EvoSuite and Randoop (Java) and Pynguin
(Python) as our own baselines (amendments~v1.1/v1.2), under an equal generation-time budget
per function/module rather than an equal number of LLM calls, since the two families of tool
are not naturally comparable on a per-call basis.

\subsection{Closing the one-shot gap: repair loops, retrieval, and context}
A substantial part of the literature is explicitly organised around \emph{fixing} the
weaknesses that plain one-shot/zero-shot prompting exhibits. Feedback/repair loops --- fed
compiler or test-failure output back to the model and iterated --- are the most common
technique (e.g.\ Zeng et al.'s Logic-CoT \cite{zeng2026logic} vs.\ ChatUniTest; Xue et al.'s
description-guided repair \cite{xue2025distinct}; Gu et al.'s template-based repair with an LLM fallback \cite{gu2025template}).
Retrieval-augmented approaches supply real API signatures or class skeletons instead of raw
source text, directly targeting the wrong-target/hallucinated-signature failure mode we
observe in our own Java results (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}); Liu et al.\ \cite{liu2025typeaware} use
call-graph context for this purpose, and Konstantinou et al.\ \cite{konstantinou2025yate} combine
call-graph and generation-history context. Best-of-$N$ sampling (generate several candidates, keep
the one that compiles) is used by, e.g., Silva et al.\ \cite{silva2025comparative} across a large panel of models.
All of these techniques share one property: they leave the strict single-call, single-sample
setting that our RQ1--RQ3 are designed to isolate. We deliberately do \emph{not} adopt them,
because our goal is to measure the native, unassisted capability of one-shot prompting on
medium-complexity, real-project code --- the same quantity the GAP-T statement identifies as
under-measured --- rather than the capability of a full agentic pipeline built around the
model.

\subsection{Coverage vs.\ mutation score as a validity concern}
Several studies explicitly warn that coverage alone overstates test quality, which motivates
our GAP-M and our choice to report both metrics under paired statistical tests rather than
coverage alone. Dakhela et al.\ \cite{dakhela2023} and Antal et al.\ \cite{antal2025leveraging} both report a gap between
syntactic/coverage success and semantic correctness; Jain and Le Goues \cite{jain2025testgeneval}'s 84.3\%
pass@1 alongside a 33.8\% mutation score is a direct instance of the same pattern we
reproduce on our own data (\texttt{JA-020}: 60\% branch coverage, 0\% mutation score;
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Few of the surveyed papers combine branch coverage and
mutation score under the same statistical test on a complexity-controlled sample (GAP-M);
most report one metric or the other, or report both without a shared significance test tying
them to a single hypothesis.

\subsection{Positioning}
Taken together, the surveyed literature establishes that LLM test generation performs well
on simple, curated problems (Section~2.1) but degrades substantially on real, uncurated code
(Section~2.2), and that the community's response has largely been architectural --- feedback
loops, retrieval, multi-sample selection (Section~2.4) --- rather than a controlled
measurement of \emph{where}, along a complexity axis, one-shot generation stops being
reliable. No paper in our evidence table isolates cyclomatic complexity as the controlled
variable across a matched Java/Python sample with both coverage and mutation score under a
shared statistical test (GAP-T/GAP-M/GAP-D, confirmed against 34, 13, and 4 supporting papers
respectively in our systematic review). This is precisely the gap RQ1--RQ3 are designed to
fill: not another demonstration that one-shot LLM tests can fail on real code, but a
controlled measurement, within a fixed medium-complexity band, of exactly how they fail and
how that compares, function-for-function, against non-LLM baselines under the same
pass/fail and quality criteria.
