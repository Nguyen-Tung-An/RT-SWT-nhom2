Related Work

Three gaps kept coming up as we read through the literature. We call them GAP-T, GAP-M,
and GAP-D. GAP-T is the one that shaped our design the most: plain zero- or one-shot
GPT-4-family generation is rarely measured at the function level, no study covers both
Java and Python together, and none of them tracks where along the complexity axis
performance starts to break down. We needed that information before we could decide how
to set up our own study. GAP-M is narrower: coverage and fault-detection ability are
seldom analysed together in a single statistical test on a controlled sample. GAP-D
follows from the other two - without complexity constraints, datasets tend to be
small, synthetic, or skewed toward easy functions. The rest of this section is organised
around these three gaps.

LLM-based test generation on curated benchmarks

A large share of the positive results in this area come from curated, self-contained
benchmarks. Wang et al. [ref] introduce TestEval - Python
LeetCode-style tasks - and report up to 98.65% line coverage and 97.16% branch
coverage for GPT-4o. Kumar et al. [ref] get 99.05% branch coverage
on HumanEval-Java using Gemini, GPT-4o, and Claude-3.5. Lira
et al. [ref] find a 90.69% success rate for Gemini 1.5-Pro on
HumanEval when both source and docstring are given. Chudic and
Calkl [ref] and Jasti et al. [ref]
push coverage even higher on similar tasks. The numbers are hard to argue with - on
these benchmarks, at least.

These numbers are impressive. But they come from short, single functions solved in
isolation - the models never have to deal with real project structure, imports, or
cross-module dependencies. None of these studies controls or reports cyclomatic
complexity, so we cannot tell at which point performance starts to drop. The benchmarks
are also public and widely used, which means many tasks may have appeared in the models'
training data. After reading through these results, our reaction was not "LLMs are
great at testing" but rather "LLMs are great at solving programming puzzles." That is
a different thing entirely.

To study that directly, we built a dataset of 120 functions from ten pinned open-source
repositories, restricted to a medium complexity band (5 <= CC <= 10,
measured with Lizard). We did not tune this range against our own results - it was
fixed in the proposal, before any test was generated. The reasoning: below CC 5 sits
mostly trivial one- or two-branch code that any tool handles, and above CC 10 sits the
extreme territory for which our survey found no complexity-controlled, GPT-4-family
numbers at all. The 5-10 band is the regime the literature leaves unmeasured.

Degradation on real-world code

Move from curated puzzles to real projects, and the picture changes fast. Haratian
et al. [ref] find GPT-4o compiling in only 37% of Java pull
requests - fail-to-pass at 13%, versus EvoSuite's 36% on the same PRs. Sapozhnikov
et al. [ref] report more than half of ChatGPT-generated Java
tests are malformed or non-compiling, partly because prompt-size limits force dropped
context. Zhang et al.'s TestBench [ref] puts Java compile-error
rates at 47.9-55.9% across nine open-source projects for CodeLlama, GPT-3.5, and
GPT-4. Konstantinou et al. [ref] show a plain zero-shot pipeline
- gpt-4o-mini among the models - still producing many non-compiling or
non-passing tests across six Java projects, with an aggregate mutation score down to
33.82%. On Python, even Jain and Le Goues's TestForge - an agentic, feedback-driven pipeline
built on GPT-4o - reaches an 84.3% pass@1 on TestGenEval while its mutation score
stays at 33.8% [ref].

Each of these studies stresses a different failure mode: compilation errors, malformed
tests, or weak mutation scores. The direction is consistent. The numbers are not. Each
group chose its own repositories, prompts, and pass criteria, so placing the results on
a common scale turned out to be harder than we expected. When we tried to do that while
designing our study, almost every individual decision looked reasonable - but the
results still could not be compared directly. We find Haratian
et al. [ref] particularly informative, because they compare GPT-4o
and EvoSuite on the same pull requests and still see GPT-4o far behind on both
compilation and fail-to-pass effectiveness.

Our own numbers are at the pessimistic end. Java: 91.7% invalid-or-no-touch.
Python: 46.7% invalid. We count a suite as effective only if it both compiles and
executes at least one line of the intended target function. A suite that compiles
against the wrong method counts as zero. We saw this often enough that it stopped
feeling like an edge case. The first time it happened, the suite looked completely
plausible - clean compilation, reasonable test names. Only when we checked coverage
and mutation did we realise it had never touched the target method at all.

Automated (non-LLM) baselines

The main non-LLM baselines are EvoSuite [ref],
Randoop [ref], and Pynguin [ref]. EvoSuite
uses genetic search and is the standard SBST baseline on
Defects4J [ref]. Randoop uses
feedback-directed random testing. Pynguin fills the same role for Python with the
DYNAMOSA algorithm. None of them relies on
natural-language prompting, which is why compile-time failures simply do not show up in
their scores.

One thing we had to get right before any comparison made sense was the time budget. With
default settings, EvoSuite looked significantly better than Randoop. The margin was
larger than we expected. When we checked, EvoSuite had 60 seconds and Randoop had 10. We
standardised everything: 60 seconds per class for EvoSuite and Randoop, 90 seconds per
module for Pynguin, set per class (Java) or per module (Python) rather than per call. In the full run,
with equal budgets, EvoSuite still led - 55.0 versus 21.1 median branch coverage. Without that fix,
we would have been measuring configuration choices, not the tools themselves.

Closing the one-shot gap: repair loops, retrieval, and context

When plain prompting fails, the common response is to add machinery around the model.
Repair loops feed compiler errors or test failures back to the model and ask it to try
again - Logic-CoT [ref], template-based repair [ref],
and YATE [ref] all follow this pattern, while
DISTINCT [ref] steers regeneration through description-guided
consistency analysis rather than raw compiler output. Retrieval-augmented approaches supply real API signatures or class
skeletons from call-graph context instead of raw source [ref].
Best-of-N sampling just generates multiple candidates and keeps whichever compiles.

These systems work. But they make it hard to know what the model is actually
contributing, versus what the scaffolding is doing. We wanted to know what happens with
no scaffolding at all - plain prompting, one sample, no repair. That is a simpler
setting than any of the pipelines above. It is also the missing baseline. If we do not
know how one-shot generation performs on medium-complexity real code, we cannot tell how
much the pipelines are actually helping.

Coverage vs.\ mutation score as a validity concern

JA-008 was the function that made this problem concrete for us. Branch
coverage: 60%. Mutation score: 0%. We spent time checking whether something had broken
in our pipeline. Nothing had. The baseline suite was executing the function - it just
never checked whether the output was correct. No assertions that mattered.

Moradi Dakhel et al. [ref] and Antal et al. [ref] both
describe this pattern: high coverage, low mutation score, tests that touch lines without
catching bugs. The sharpest published instance we found is TestForge's 84.3% pass@1 sitting alongside
a 33.8% mutation score [ref] - and that is an agentic pipeline,
not one-shot prompting.
JA-008 is ours.

Most prior work reports one metric or the other, or puts them side by side without tying
them together statistically. That makes it hard to say whether LLM-generated tests are
actually useful or just good at executing code. We use both together - a threshold
test plus per-function comparisons against baselines - on the same function IDs as in
RQ1.

Positioning

Reading across everything we surveyed, three things stand out. On curated benchmarks,
results are strong - near-perfect coverage on TestEval and
HumanEval [ref]. On real
projects, they are not - Java compilation success often below about
50% [ref], and mutation scores far below the
coverage numbers. And most of the real gains in recent work come from the surrounding
system - repair loops, retrieval, selection - not from better raw
generation [ref].

What that leaves open is the question we actually care about: how does plain one-shot
generation perform on medium-complexity, real-project code, measured consistently across
languages and metrics? Nobody has answered that yet. RQ1-RQ3 are our attempt. The setup
is deliberately simple - one prompt, one sample, fixed complexity range, same metrics
for Java and Python. Simple enough to reason about. Close enough to practice that the
failures should matter.
